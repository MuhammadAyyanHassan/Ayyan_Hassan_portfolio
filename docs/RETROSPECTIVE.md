# FlyRank AI Fluency — Final Retrospective

## Written for my Week 1 self

In Week 1, I approached the portfolio as a website I needed to build and present. My initial goal was fairly direct: create a personal portfolio that could introduce me, show my work, and give someone a way to contact me. As the work progressed, that goal became more demanding. The portfolio stopped being only a visual exercise and became a small product that needed a clear information structure, evidence for the work being presented, reproducible implementation, testing, and a documented explanation of the decisions behind it.

One of the first important changes was how I chose the technology. Instead of asking AI to choose a framework and accepting the first answer, I defined constraints around cost, my existing skills, what the portfolio actually needed to display, and whether it needed dynamic functionality. I compared plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript, Astro, and Next.js + TypeScript. The final decision was Next.js + TypeScript on Vercel because it gave me reusable component structure and room to grow while still allowing the current portfolio to remain mostly static. That decision also taught me that a more powerful tool is not automatically the right tool; it has to be justified by the actual requirements.

The visual direction changed in a similar way. Rather than continuously adding decoration, I settled on a deliberately restrained identity: Space Grotesk for headings, Inter for body text, and a complete palette of Deep Burgundy (#6B1F2A), Ink (#171717), and Warm White (#F7F5F2). The intended mood is quietly technical and deliberate, with typography, spacing, and restraint carrying the identity. This gave later implementation and critique a stable reference point instead of letting every page become a separate design experiment.

The biggest change in my process was learning to treat the live result as the thing that needed to be tested. The portfolio went through a hardening pass rather than stopping when the pages looked correct. I checked form validation and edge cases, rapid submission, special characters and Unicode, API method behavior, narrow layouts, links, findability, social preview behavior, PageSpeed evidence, and mobile behavior. That process produced concrete fixes rather than only observations. The final hardening review recorded a PASS, while still keeping limitations visible. In particular, the contact endpoint does not yet have a dedicated rate-limiting or anti-spam layer. I learned that a credible final result is not one with no limitations; it is one where the limitations are known and stated clearly.

If I continued the project, I would improve the portfolio beyond the scope of this checkpoint rather than treating this submission as the final version forever. I would strengthen production hardening around the contact endpoint, continue improving the case-study presentation, and add richer interactions only where they improve communication rather than decoration. I would also keep the underlying structure reusable so new projects can be added without rebuilding the site from scratch.

Three lessons from this build are especially transferable. **First, define constraints before asking AI for solutions.** The quality of an AI-assisted decision depends heavily on the problem definition and the alternatives being pressure-tested. **Second, test the real artifact, not just the implementation.** A successful build is not the same as a reliable user experience; the live site has to be exercised against realistic and awkward cases. **Third, treat AI as a partner rather than an authority.** AI helped with planning, implementation, critique, and documentation, but the final decisions and checks remained mine. That combination—clear constraints, direct verification, and deliberate use of AI—is the part of this project I would carry into the next one.
